## 1. Definition

Wilding conifers (wilding pines) are self-seeded exotic conifer trees growing where they are not wanted across the New Zealand landscape through natural regeneration. The term is specific to New Zealand. They are described as "the wrong tree in the wrong place" - weeds classified as the biggest threat to New Zealand's unique environment, comparable to stoats, rats, and possums.

Planted, well-managed conifers provide shelter, recreation, and income opportunities. The problem arises when these species self-seed and spread uncontrolled into farmland, native ecosystems, and sensitive water catchments.

*Sources: [1], [2], [4]*

## 2. Species

Ten introduced conifer species are responsible for most wilding spread in New Zealand:

### 2.1 Contorta Pine (*Pinus contorta*) - Most Aggressive
- Also known as lodgepole pine
- Most aggressive spreader; youngest coning age; widest spread
- Produces viable seed as young as 8 years old
- **Declared an unwanted organism** under the Biosecurity Act 1993 (since 2001) - cannot be bred, propagated, distributed, or sold in NZ
- The only species on the prohibited list that is trade-prohibited
- Considered the most invasive wilding conifer species in Aotearoa

### 2.2 Douglas Fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*)
- Second most common commercial timber species in NZ
- Spreads rapidly in montane areas
- Present at approximately 25% of DOC-administered infestation sites in the South Island
- Highly invasive despite also being commercially valuable

### 2.3 Radiata Pine (*Pinus radiata*)
- Most common commercial timber species in NZ
- Spreads in lowland areas, affecting native bush regeneration
- Present at over half of South Island infestation sites threatening DOC land
- Planting is a **discretionary activity** under QLDC District Plan (not prohibited), except where NES-PF prevails

### 2.4 Ponderosa Pine (*Pinus ponderosa*)
- Historically grown as amenity tree in very dry areas
- Viable seed at approximately 13 years
- Often spreads from amenity plantings

### 2.5 Scots Pine (*Pinus sylvestris*)
- Bluish tinge
- Rarely grown for timber
- Spread from early trial sites

### 2.6 Bishop Pine (*Pinus muricata*)
- Rarely grown for timber
- Spread from early trial sites

### 2.7 Maritime Pine (*Pinus pinaster*)
- Very large tree
- Grows with radiata pine in coastal areas
- Viable seed at approximately 10 years

### 2.8 Corsican Pine (*Pinus nigra*)
- Slower maturation than contorta (seed at approximately 13 years)
- Spreads very large distances
- Present at approximately 14% of DOC sites

### 2.9 Dwarf Mountain Pine (*Pinus mugo*) and Mountain Pine (*Pinus mugo uncinata*)
- Short, bushy species planted in alpine areas
- Slow spread but extremely difficult to eliminate
- Can establish above the native treeline
- Mature early, seed at approximately 8 years

### 2.10 European Larch (*Larix decidua*)
- Distinctive deciduous conifer
- Invasive in wetter areas
- Viable seed at approximately 12 years
- Present at approximately 16% of DOC sites

### Additional Prohibited Species (QLDC District Plan)
The QLDC District Plan Rule 34.4.2 also prohibits planting of additional exotic species beyond the 10 primary conifers:
- Sycamore (*Acer pseudoplatanus*)
- Hawthorn (*Crataegus monogyna*)
- Boxthorn (*Lycium ferocissimum*)
- Buddleia (*Buddleja davidii*)
- Grey willow (*Salix cinerea*)
- Crack willow (*Salix fragilis*)
- Cotoneaster (*Simonsii*)
- Rowan (*Sorbus aucuparia*)
- Spanish heath (*Erica lusitanica*)

### Seed Dispersal Characteristics
- Seeds have an attached "wing" enabling dispersal over large distances in high winds
- Mature conifer cones open to release wind-blown seeds travelling kilometres downwind
- Seeds require no special conditions for germination
- Uncontrolled stands can reach densities of 10,000 stems per hectare (vs 600-1,100 for managed plantation)

### Timber Quality
Wilding conifers are typically malformed with large branches, pronounced taper, mixed-species grouping, and inaccessible locations, making timber extraction economically unviable.

*Sources: [4], [5], [2]*

## 3. History

- **1880s:** Conifers first widely introduced to New Zealand, most originating from North America and Europe. Planted for erosion control, shelter, timber, and land stabilisation.
- **Late 1800s:** Botanist Thomas Cheeseman noted seven introduced tree species reproducing "very freely" across the country.
- **Early 1900s:** Contemporary authors already describing some conifer species as invasive. Conifers planted around Skippers cemetery (Queenstown area) circa 1880 - these Douglas firs subsequently spread across the landscape.
- **1900s-1950s:** Period responsible for most plantings that are the source of today's wilding problem. Conifers planted at Flock Hill Station (Canterbury) in the mid- to late-1900s for erosion control and provenance trials.
- **1950s onward:** Significant areas of wilding spread first observed.
- **1970s:** Rapid infestation noted from this decade onwards.
- **1975:** First wilding conifers noted at Flock Hill Station.
- **2001:** *Pinus contorta* declared an unwanted organism under the Biosecurity Act 1993.
- **2007:** Wilding conifer area estimated at approximately 805,000 hectares in the South Island and 300,000 hectares in the North Island. Approximately $6 million spent directly on control across central and local government.
- **~2010:** Volunteers in Whakatipu began preventing seed dispersal from mature Douglas fir forests.
- **2011:** MPI commissioned Pacific Eco-Logic to produce an independent status report on wilding conifers.
- **2014:** DOC estimates including sparse spread reached approximately 1.7 million hectares. 5% densely populated (>400 stems/ha), 20% moderately populated (20-400 stems/ha), 75% sparsely populated (<20 stems/ha).
- **2015:** National Wilding Conifer Management Strategy 2015-2030 published.
- **2016:** National Wilding Conifer Control Programme (NWCCP) established.

*Sources: [4], [7], [10], [1]*

## 4. Scale of the Problem

- **Current extent:** More than 2 million hectares infested by wilding conifers (MPI 2024 figure; earlier estimates cited 1.8 million hectares)
- **Spread rate:** Approximately 5% per year — approximately 90,000 hectares per year before the NWCCP was established
- **Potential coverage:** An estimated 25% of New Zealand would be covered within 30 years if left unchecked. Over the next 50 years, wildings would spread to a further 500,000 hectares and 1.8 million hectares would become dense forest (Manaaki Whenua modelling)
- **Predicted economic cost:** Rolling back funding to $10M/year would result in losses of **$3.8 billion over 50 years** through losses in primary production, water yields, biodiversity, cultural values, and increased fire damage
- **Operations completed:** The NWCCP has completed operations on 3 million hectares (11% of vulnerable land). Approximately 80% of total known national infestation area has received at least one control round since 2016
- **Annual cost escalation:** Control costs increase 30% with each year of delay
- **Density:** Uncontrolled stands reach up to 10,000 stems per hectare
- **Geographic concentration:** Predominantly South Island - Canterbury high country, Otago, Mackenzie Basin, Marlborough, Southland. Also Central Plateau (North Island), Coromandel, Northland
- **Management units:** New Zealand is divided into **129 management units** for wilding conifer control (49 were active at the time of the 2022 Sapere report)

### Density Classes
The national programme classifies infestations by density:
- **Outlier:** 0-1% canopy cover — 2% production loss
- **Sparse:** 1-15% canopy cover — 20% production loss
- **Intermediate:** 15-75% canopy cover — 30% production loss
- **Dense:** 75-100% canopy cover — 100% production loss

Approximately 80% of current infestation is sparse, 15% intermediate, 3.5% outlier, and 1.5% dense. Left unmanaged, large areas of sparse infestation would become dense forest within 30 years — in some cases as little as 14 to 21 years for *Pinus contorta*.

## 5. Environmental Impacts

### 5.1 Native Ecosystems and Biodiversity
- Dense growth blocks sunlight, water, and nutrients from other plants
- Creates monoculture forests supporting primarily other wilding pines
- Native plants and animals forced out
- Particularly damaging to low-stature native plant communities: tussock grasslands, alpine/subalpine, geothermal, coastal areas
- Several species establish above the natural treeline, threatening alpine ecosystems with no native tree competitors
- Once canopy closes, ecosystems change fundamentally and irreversibly

### 5.2 Water Resources
- When pastoral land becomes densely infested with wilding conifers, annual water yield reductions of **30-81%** have been measured
- Scion found an average reduction of approximately **16% during low-flow conditions** across three South Island catchments
- The WATYIELD model (Fahey et al., 2010) showed a **40% reduction in mean annual flow** with two-thirds of an experimental catchment planted in pines
- Key hydro catchments at risk: Clutha, Manapouri, Tongariro, Waikaremoana, Waikato, Waitaki
- Irrigation benefits from wilding control account for approximately 24% of total economic benefits; hydro benefits account for a further significant share
- Release water vapour affecting atmospheric conditions

### 5.3 Soil
- Soil acidification through needle litter, leading to mineral depletion
- Disrupt soil composition and nutrient levels, inhibiting native plant growth
- Alter nutrient cycling patterns

### 5.4 Fire Risk
- Wilding pines are highly flammable
- Dense infestations significantly increase wildfire risk
- Dense, impenetrable growth creates extreme fire hazard
- The NWCCP works with Fire and Emergency New Zealand (FENZ) on wildfire risk management where wilding pines present dangerous fuel loads
- The December 2024 Bridge Hill wildfire was specifically linked to wilding pine fuel loads

### 5.5 Pest Habitat
- Provide habitat for exotic pests and diseases

### 5.6 Post-Removal Legacy Effects
Research by Dickie et al. (2022) found that wilding conifers cause potentially irreversible ecosystem changes:
- Soil nitrogen and phosphorus availability increases persist after removal
- These changes promote exotic herb/grass invasion post-removal
- Native tussock grasslands prove unsustainable on most invaded sites without continuous intervention
- Native woody vegetation offers greater sustainability potential for restoration
- Shade-tolerant species (e.g., Douglas fir) pose continued reinvasion risks even after control

*Sources: [4], [2], [12]*

## 6. Economic Impacts

- **Total projected cost:** $4.6 billion in losses if unchecked (some analyses estimate $5-6.3 billion over 50 years)
- **Impacts on:** Primary production, biodiversity, hydroelectric generation, irrigation
- **Farming:** Reduces viability of high country pastoral farming by displacing grazing land. On extensive high country, control costs can exceed economic return from the land.
- **Tourism and landscape:** Threatens iconic NZ landscapes underpinning tourism (Central Otago, Mackenzie Country, Canterbury high country)
- **Water-dependent industries:** Reduced water yield threatens irrigation agriculture and hydroelectric power
- **Land values:** Infested land loses productive and amenity value; limits future land-use options
- **Productive land values:** Low-producing grassland earns $52.89/ha/year (EBITR); high-producing grassland earns $344.45/ha/year

### Sapere Cost-Benefit Analysis (2022)

A comprehensive cost-benefit analysis prepared by Sapere for MPI (September 2022) assessed four investment scenarios over 50 years:

| Scenario | Management Units | % Infestation Controlled | Benefit:Cost Ratio | Net Present Value |
|----------|-----------------|-------------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Status quo ($10M/yr) | 10 | 42.4% | 20:1 | $1.9 billion |
| Minimum "protect the investment" | 49 | 90.0% | 34:1 | $5.7 billion |
| Intermediate "extend the investment" | 60 | 94.8% | 33:1 | $6.3 billion |
| Maximum "national control" | 79 | 95.1% | 32:1 | $6.7 billion |

**Losses under status quo** (rolling back to $10M/year — present value over 50 years):
- Productive land use: -$1,328M
- Hydro generation: -$373M
- Irrigation: -$695M
- Biodiversity and cultural values: -$1,262M
- Fire damage: -$222M
- **Total losses: -$3.8 billion**

**Benefits of minimum investment** (present value over 50 years):
- Productive land use: $1,967M
- Hydro generation: $307M
- Irrigation: $1,160M
- Cultural/biodiversity: $2,137M
- Fire risk reduction: $308M
- **Total benefits: $5,879M**

### Control Cost Per Hectare
- Initial control ranges from **$100 to $3,000 per hectare** depending on density, terrain, and native vegetation sensitivity
- $100/ha for low-density, flat land, large trees, no native vegetation
- $3,000/ha for high-density with sensitive surrounding vegetation
- Ground control of scattered infestations by hand is much cheaper than dense infestations requiring heavy machinery or aerial control

### Density Class Transition Timelines (With Control)
With control applied every 3 years:
- **Dense → cleared:** 12 years (Dense → Sparse at 6yr → Outlier at 9yr → None at 12yr)
- **Intermediate → cleared:** 9 years
- **Sparse → cleared:** 6 years
- **Outlier → cleared:** 3 years

### Economic Valuation Research (Lincoln University)
A choice experiment study applied economic valuation to community preferences regarding wilding pine impacts on indigenous species in the South Island high country. The high country was conceptualised as natural capital comprising an ecological system, with wilding tree incursion changing the flow of ecosystem services. Areas with high biodiversity were ranked highest priority for management investment.

## 7. Management and Control

### 7.1 National Wilding Conifer Control Programme (NWCCP)
- Established 2016, led by Biosecurity New Zealand (within MPI)
- Partners: regional councils, unitary authorities, DOC, LINZ, community groups, landowners, researchers, industry
- Has completed operations on 3 million hectares (11% of vulnerable land)
- Two primary control methods: ground control and aerial control

### 7.2 Control Methods Overview

Control methods divide into two categories: **ground control** and **aerial control**. Ground control can be manual labour or mechanical. Aerial control uses helicopters or drones. Choice of technique depends on the size of the area, density of the infestation, tree size, species, terrain/access, surrounding vegetation, future land-use plans, and budget.

**Key Rules of Thumb (from Wilding Pine Control Guidelines):**
- **Early control** when trees are small is always best — it's the lowest cost
- **Prioritise seed sources** — remove upwind areas first; if cleared first, they are least likely to be reinvaded
- **Ongoing maintenance** is essential — pine seeds are viable in soil for 5-6 years, so one round of control is not enough. Do an initial round then maintenance every 2-3 years until no more seedlings emerge
- **Control costs increase 30%** with each year of delay
- You must use the right herbicide for the species and control method

### 7.3 Ground Control Methods (Sparse to Moderate Densities)

**Hand-Pulling / Hand-Saws / Loppers:**
- **When used:** Seedlings and saplings at scattered density; not suitable for adult trees or dense infestations
- **How it's done:** Small seedlings (<2 years old) can be pulled up at the base. Larger seedlings are cut at the base — crucial to remove all green needles so the tree won't regrow. Apply chemical to the cut stump (see Cut Stump method)
- **Tools:** Gloves, hand-saw or secateurs/loppers
- **Safety:** Care with hand-saws; proper pulling technique required
- **Comments:** Preserves quality of surrounding native vegetation the most. Best done in moist soil conditions. Suitable for volunteer days but requires committed participants

**Chainsaws / Felling:**
- **When used:** Medium to large trees at scattered to moderate density on readily accessed terrain
- **How it's done:** Trees felled with chainsaw; small axe useful for ensuring all green foliage is removed close to ground
- **Tools:** Chainsaws, associated safety and felling equipment
- **Safety:** Only for trained, experienced people with appropriate safety gear. Must adhere to Approved Code of Practice for Forestry
- **Comments:** Not recommended for large trees surrounded by native bush/shrubland — felling creates light-gaps where new wildings establish and can damage surrounding vegetation. Use Drill and Fill technique instead in such situations

**Scrub Bar:**
- **When used:** Wide range of wilding tree sizes, usually more effective at higher densities. Most useful on relatively flat terrain with few stones
- **How it's done:** Can cut down trees up to 15cm diameter at the base. Can be used in conjunction with chainsaws for larger trees
- **Tools:** Scrub-bar, tungsten-tipped blades, sharpening gear, safety equipment
- **Safety:** Training required; not recommended for inexperienced people

**Cut Stump with Herbicide Application:**
- **When used:** Small to medium-sized trees
- **How it's done:** Tree is felled or cut, then herbicide is applied directly to the freshly cut stump surface to prevent regrowth
- **Ensures:** Trees don't resprout from the stump

**Drill and Fill:**
- **When used:** Small to medium to large-sized trees. Especially suitable for areas with sensitive surrounding vegetation
- **How it's done:** Holes drilled into the tree trunk, then filled with herbicide. Tree dies standing, avoiding damage to surrounding native vegetation from felling
- **Comments:** Preferred method when large wildings are surrounded by native bush — avoids creating light-gaps

**GBBA (Ground-Based Basal Bark Application):**
- **When used:** Small to medium-sized trees
- **How it's done:** Herbicide applied directly to the bark at the base of the tree
- **Comments:** Effective targeted method for accessible trees

**Mechanical Control (Digger/Bulldozer/Mulcher):**
- **When used:** Moderate to dense infestations
- **How it's done:** Mechanical control using digger or mulcher. Some large machines can handle large-sized trees
- **Comments:** Enables conversion to pasture but can make native restoration more costly because it may be harder for native plants to establish

**Burning:**
- **When used:** Medium to large-sized trees at moderate to dense infestations
- **Safety:** Only by experienced professionals who have obtained all permits and consents
- **Comments:** Requires careful fire management planning

### 7.4 Aerial Control Methods

**ABBA (Aerial Basal Bark Application):**
- **When used:** Sparse to moderate densities of scattered individual trees
- **How it's done:** Helicopter-based targeted herbicide application to the bark of individual trees
- **Comments:** Precision method for inaccessible terrain with scattered wildings

**AFSA (Aerial Foliar Spray Application):**
- **When used:** Dense wilding infestations
- **How it's done:** Broad helicopter-based herbicide spraying over foliage of dense stands
- **Comments:** Most cost-effective for large-scale dense infestations. Leaves dead standing forests for 10-15 years while they naturally decay. Less than 1% of all herbicide application involves aerial foliar spraying

**Skid-Hopping:**
- **When used:** Large-sized trees at sparse densities
- **How it's done:** Helicopter lands near each tree (skid-hops), crew dismounts to fell/treat the tree, then moves to next

**Drone-Based Precision Spraying:**
- Being trialled for scattered or inaccessible trees using minimal herbicide
- UAV exploration for moderate-density infestations under research

**Low-Dose Herbicide Innovation:**
- The national programme developed and tested low-dose herbicides for dense wilding invasion
- Reduces chemicals used while effectively controlling over 95% of wilding trees
- Programme supported trials of new control methods to reduce costs and enable control in locations where existing methods are not practical or safe

### 7.5 Detection Methods

- Automated algorithms distinguishing dark-green conifers from grassland backgrounds
- UAV and manned aircraft multispectral and LiDAR data for early detection
- Satellite remote sensing using Landsat archive for monitoring spread trends
- Research into detecting conifers prior to coning onset using ultra-high-resolution data (Canterbury PhD)

### 7.6 Prevention Methods

- Windward slope plantation placement (revised from previous hilltop assumptions)
- Sheep grazing for seedling control
- Fertiliser application enhancing native grass competition
- Gene editing research for sterile Douglas fir development
- Wilding Tree Risk Calculator required under NES-CF before new planting

### 7.7 Post-Control Options

- **Native restoration:** Planting native species after wilding removal. More sustainable long-term but requires management of invasive grasses/exotic plants that colonise cleared areas
- **Pasture:** Conversion to grazing land. Mechanical removal enables this but can make native restoration harder
- **Plant non-spreading trees:** Replace wildings with species that don't self-seed
- **Maintain tussock grasslands:** Requires ongoing intervention due to soil legacy effects

### 7.8 Decision Factors Flowchart

Factors affecting **control method choice:** slope, access, area, future use, hindrance (boulders, vegetation density, rivers/streams), native vegetation

Factors affecting **control costs:** slope, hindrance (initial costs), species/density/tree size (initial costs), coning/seed bank/species/seed rain (follow-up costs), area (total costs/ha)

*Reference: Flowchart from Edwards, P., Sprague, R., and Stahlmann-Brown, P. 2021. Removing invasive conifers - considerations, complexity and costs. Environmental Research Communications, 3, 071004.*

## 8. Legislation and Policy

### 8.1 Biosecurity Act 1993
- *Pinus contorta* declared an unwanted organism (2001) - cannot be bred, propagated, distributed, or sold
- Regional Pest Management Plans enable regional councils to manage wilding spread

### 8.2 Resource Management Act (RMA)
- National Environmental Standards for Commercial Forestry (NES-CF), effective 3 November 2023 (previously NES for Plantation Forestry, effective 1 May 2018)
- From 1 April 2024: requires wilding tree risk assessment before afforestation/replanting
- Uses Wilding Tree Risk Calculator: score <12 = permitted; score >= 12 = requires resource consent
- Requires eradication of resulting wilding conifers at least every 5 years
- Gives councils more power over where new forests can be located

### 8.3 NZ Wilding Conifer Management Strategy 2015-2030
- Non-statutory strategy: "The right tree in the right place" (ISBN: 978-0-477-10511-8)
- Published December 2014, championed by the NZ Wilding Conifer Management Group (NZWCMG)
- Developed by MPI with a multi-stakeholder working group including DOC, LINZ, NZ Defence Force, regional councils, Scion, NZ Forest Owners Association, Federated Farmers, and community groups
- Vision: prevent spread and contain/eradicate established areas by 2030
- Four principles: (1) recognise individual and collective responsibilities; (2) be cost-effective and timely; (3) prioritise; (4) co-ordinate
- Strategy notes that two of the ten most spread-prone species (radiata pine and Douglas fir) make up **96% of the plantation estate** and are principal contributors to NZ's **$4.3 billion forestry exports** (third largest export earner after dairy and meat)
- Original plantings that caused the problem date back two to three generations, planted by private occupiers, government organisations (catchment boards, NZ Forest Service, National Water and Soil Conservation Organisation)

### 8.4 QLDC District Plan - Chapter 34: Wilding Exotic Trees
- **Purpose:** Prevent spread of wilding exotic trees to protect landscape, biodiversity, water, and soil values
- **Objective 34.2.1:** Protection of the District's landscape, biodiversity, water and soil resource values from the spread of wilding exotic trees
- **Rule 34.4.1:** Planting of Radiata pine is a **Discretionary** activity (except where NES-PF prevails)
- **Rule 34.4.2:** Planting of all 10 primary wilding conifer species plus 8 additional exotic species is **Prohibited** - no application for resource consent can be accepted
- **Policy 34.2.1.2:** Any Radiata pine planting must be effectively and practicably managed to avoid wilding spread
- **Policy 34.2.1.3:** Radiata pine proposals must consider: location/wind exposure, surrounding land uses, land ownership constraints, management plans, and risk assessment results
- **Exemption 34.4.3:** Rules do not require felling/removal of any tree identified as protected in the District Plan
- Rules apply district-wide, including formed and unformed roads, whether zoned or not
- The Council also manages wildings through the non-statutory "Wakatipu Wilding Conifer Strategy"

### 8.5 Wakatipu Wilding Conifer Strategy
- Non-statutory document used by QLDC
- Manages existing wilding trees in the Wakatipu area
- Works alongside District Plan rules that control planting of wilding potential species

## 10. Scientific Research

### 10.1 Seed Predation by Exotic Mammals (Carlin et al. 2024)

**Citation:** Carlin, T.F., Paul, T.S.H., Dudenhoeffer, J.H., Rolando, C., Novoselov, M., Vorster, R.S., Springford, C.R. & Scott, M.B. (2024). The enemy of my enemy... Exotic mammals present biotic resistance against invasive alien conifers. *Biological Invasions*. DOI: 10.1007/s10530-024-03336-z

**Institutions:** Scion (Christchurch & Rotorua), Lincoln University

**Study Site:** Flock Hill Station, Canterbury - a 14,500 ha working sheep and cattle station containing indigenous tussock grasslands, forest, improved pasture, invasive pine forest, alpine scrub, and beech forest.

**Species Studied:** *Pinus contorta* (lodgepole pine) and *Pseudotsuga menziesii* (Douglas fir)

**Methodology:**
- 45 plots distributed across 5 habitat types: tussock grassland (*Chionochloa* spp.), improved pasture, aerially-controlled conifer forest (ACCF), ground-controlled conifer areas (GCCA), and beech (*Nothofagus fusca*) forest
- 2,700 seeds per habitat type (2,025 *P. contorta*, 675 *Ps. menziesii*)
- Seeds individually glued to 10cm wooden popsicle sticks, pressed into ground with 1cm above surface
- Plots checked every 2 weeks for 6 weeks (late summer, March 2023)
- Seed density gradients tested: low (50cm apart), medium (10cm), high (2cm)
- Feeding stations with trail cameras (Moultrie M-Series) in each habitat
- Ex-situ validation at Willowbank Wildlife Park (possum, wallaby enclosures) and Scion office site (rats, mice, birds)
- Damage categories: undamaged, hollowed out, missing, damaged, stick damaged (further split by possum/sheep/cattle)

**Key Results - Seed Predation Rates by Habitat:**

| Habitat | *P. contorta* | *Ps. menziesii* |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| Beech Forest | 92.8% | 95.9% |
| ACCF | 91.7% | 82.7% |
| Pasture | 90.7% | 72.4% |
| GCCA | 70.0% | 69.8% |
| **Tussock** | **52.1%** | **31.6%** |

- **Tussock grassland had the lowest seed predation** - making it the most vulnerable habitat for conifer invasion
- Majority of seed predation occurred within the first 2 weeks
- No significant overall difference between *P. contorta* and *Ps. menziesii* predation rates, but significant interactions showed *Ps. menziesii* had lower predation in pasture and tussock
- Smaller *P. contorta* seeds (~4 mg) were more commonly predated than larger *Ps. menziesii* seeds (~12.5 mg)
- Close (2 cm) seed aggregation had significantly lower predation than 50 cm or 10 cm spacing

**Seed Predator Identification:**
- 824 images/videos captured, 152 unique visits at Flock Hill
- **Rodents** (mice and rats): 126 unique visits - by far the most common seed predators
- **Birds:** 13 unique visits (all introduced species; 5 total feeding events)
- **Possums:** 8 unique visits
- **Hedgehogs:** 5 unique visits (no seed predation suspected)
- Neither native nor introduced birds significantly contributed to seed predation in any habitat
- Hedgehogs, deer, and pigs present at Flock Hill but not suspected of predating seeds
- Wallabies did not show preference for eating conifer seed

**Missing seeds** were the most common damage type (rodents). Possum damage was the second most common category. Hollow seeds attributed to invertebrates. Rodents responsible for "missing" and "damaged" categories; possums less careful, causing major stick damage.

**Critical Implications:**
- Introduced mammal control operations (essential for protecting native species) will likely result in **increasing invasive conifer populations** by reducing seed predation pressure
- Few, if any, native species provide significant seed predation on wilding conifers
- This creates a management paradox: Predator Free 2050 goals could inadvertently accelerate wilding conifer spread
- Tussock grasslands are most vulnerable because they support fewer vertebrate seed predators
- Forested habitats (beech, ACCF) have highest predation due to higher predator abundances
- ACCF had twice as many feeding events as beech forest despite similar visitation, suggesting food scarcity drives more active seed consumption
- Grazing livestock in pasture likely ingest seeds passively rather than targeting them

### 10.2 Reproductive Trait Shift in *Pinus contorta* (Carlin et al. 2025)

**Citation:** Carlin, T.F., Scott, M.B. & Paul, T.S.H. (2025). Reproductive trait shift in *Pinus contorta* helps explain invasion success in Aotearoa New Zealand. *New Zealand Journal of Ecology*, 49(1), 3584.

**Institutions:** Scion (Christchurch & Rotorua)

**Key Findings:**
- Filled seeds per cone averaged **74 across six study sites** - approximately **3x the native North American range** and **50% higher than previous Aotearoa estimates**
- One site (Craigieburn, Canterbury) showed notably lower yields at 26 seeds per cone
- Average cone length: 43.6 mm (range: 15.9-62.0 mm)
- The number of seed scales per cone is similar between Aotearoa and native range, suggesting **increased fertile scale proportions** rather than cone size expansion
- This increase in seed-holding capacity has likely enhanced invasion success by increasing propagule pressure
- The trees are effectively evolving to produce more and larger cones with more seeds than in their native range

### 10.3 Complex Planting Histories and Hybridisation (Scion, 2023-2025)

**Source:** Scion Connections Issue 44 (Dec 2023); Springer *Trees* (2025)

**Key Findings:**
- All four subspecies of *P. contorta* were established almost ubiquitously across NZ
- Invasive populations are unlikely to represent any single subspecies
- An **intra-specific hybrid** between coastal and inland subspecies of *P. contorta* has been identified
- This hybrid produces **larger cones and more viable seeds** than either parent subspecies in their home ranges
- Observed trait shifts could be due to intentionally introduced hybrids or unintentional intermixing
- **Serotinous cones** (which remain closed and release seeds after fire or death) have been identified in populations across much of NZ - previously thought to be absent
- Serotinous cone banks persist within herbicide-treated populations with **no effect on seed viability**
- Despite lessening initial spread rates, cone serotiny may become more common by allowing populations to **reinvade after herbicide treatments**
- This has critical implications: herbicide-killed trees may still release viable seed
- Sampled across five regions: Kaweka Range, Molesworth, Craigieburn, Mackenzie, Mid-Dome (as part of "Vive la Resistance" programme, begun 2021)

### 10.4 Ecological Management Framework (Dickie et al. 2022)

**Citation:** Dickie, I.A., Sprague, R., Green, J., Peltzer, D.A., Orwin, K. & Sapsford, S. (2022). Applying ecological research to improve long-term outcomes of wilding conifer management. *New Zealand Journal of Ecology*, 46(2), 3475.

**Institutions:** BioProtection Aotearoa / University of Canterbury; NZ Wilding Conifer Group; Manaaki Whenua - Landcare Research

**Key Findings:**
- Management activities may not achieve desired longer-term outcomes without integrating ecological understanding
- Multiple **ecosystem tipping points** exist where prioritised management can prevent irreversible state-changes
- Post-removal legacies persist, complicating restoration
- Native tussock grasslands are **unsustainable on most invaded sites** without continuous intervention
- Native woody vegetation offers greater sustainability for restoration
- Additional management required for invasive grasses and persistent soil legacies
- Shade-tolerant species (e.g., Douglas fir) pose continued reinvasion risks
- A decision-support framework was developed guiding transitions toward three land uses: pasture, plantations, or native restoration

### 10.5 Public and Visitor Perceptions

**Edwards et al. (2020)** - "Pernicious pests and public perceptions"
- *Land Use Policy*, 97, 104759
- Authors: Peter Edwards, Philip Stahlmann-Brown (Manaaki Whenua), Simon Thomas (MPI)
- Wildings and their negative aspects increasingly recognised in NZ
- Many landowners volunteer to control wildings but need more support
- Mechanical control methods have highest public approval over spraying
- All aspects of control require careful consideration for public acceptance

**Lovelock et al. (2024)** - "Not seeing the wood for the (invasive) trees"
- *Journal of Outdoor Recreation and Tourism*, 48, 100818
- Authors: Brent Lovelock, Yun Ji, Anna Carr (University of Otago), Clara-Jane Blye (University of Alberta)
- 231 survey respondents (domestic and international visitors)
- **Awareness of the wilding conifer problem was generally low**, especially among international visitors
- Significant differences between domestic and international visitor perceptions
- International visitors, particularly from China and other Asian countries, were **more accepting of wild conifers** and less supportive of eradication
- Tourism management implications: stakeholder support needed from tourists and recreation sectors

**Otago University "Good Nature, Bad Nature" Project** (Marsden-funded)
- Explores how attitudes to invasive species are formed
- Some invasive species may be important for local communities (food, firewood, recreation, tourism)
- Communities may reject control programmes if they value the species
- Research focus on wilding conifers' effect on landscape and social impact on different groups

### 10.6 Winning Against Wildings Research Programme

**Period:** October 2017 - September 2021 (4-year MBIE Endeavour-funded programme)
**Partners:** Manaaki Whenua, Scion, Lincoln University, University of Canterbury, Australian National University, National Centre for Atmospheric Research (USA)

**Research Streams:**

1. **Control (Led by Scion):** Mechanical methods, herbicide optimisation (reduced volume maintaining efficacy), UAV exploration for moderate-density infestations

2. **Detection (Led by Scion):** Automated algorithms for distinguishing conifers from grassland; balancing cost vs accuracy in remote sensing

3. **Forecasting (Led by Manaaki Whenua):** Spatial spread modelling. Marlborough research found **containment costs increase drastically over time**, favouring upfront removal in windy areas

4. **Impacts and Legacies (Led by Manaaki Whenua/Canterbury):** Irreversible soil changes, exotic plant invasion post-removal, carbon/fire/water effects. 2014 research demonstrated potentially irreversible change through nitrogen/phosphorus changes

5. **Prevention (Led by Scion):** Windward slope placement (revised from hilltop assumptions), sheep grazing, fertiliser for native grass competition, gene editing for sterile Douglas fir

6. **Social Research (Led by Manaaki Whenua):** Rural Decision Makers surveys (2015, 2017) - 83% of affected landowners controlled wildings, citing stewardship, pasture maintenance, habitat protection

7. **Spread Risk (Led by Lincoln University):** Seed wing characteristics and within-species variation affecting dispersal distance. Research on *P. radiata* challenged assumptions that heavy seeds disperse shorter distances

**Key Contacts:** Duane Peltzer (Group Portfolio Leader), Pike Stahlmann-Brown (Principal Researcher, Economics), Norman Mason (Principal Researcher, Quantitative Ecology)

### 10.7 Remote Sensing Detection (University of Canterbury)

**Thesis:** Dash, J. (2020). On the detection and monitoring of invasive exotic conifers in New Zealand using remote sensing. PhD thesis, University of Canterbury.

- Developed methods using UAVs as emerging technology
- Investigated monitoring historic trends through Landsat archive analysis (first such research)
- Key question: Can ultra-high-resolution data detect invasive conifers **prior to the onset of early coning** in highly vulnerable environments?
- Demonstrated efficacy of pre-coning detection using high-resolution UAV and manned aircraft data
- Important tool for early intervention before trees begin producing seed

## 12. The Wakatipu Wilding Conifer Strategy

### 12.1 Overview and Governance

The **Whakatipu Wilding Control Group (WCG)** is a community-led, not-for-profit incorporated society established by QLDC in 2009. It coordinates all wilding conifer control across the Wakatipu Basin — approximately **80,000 hectares** of public and private land infested with wilding pines. The group won the **2022 New Zealand Business Awards** for its impact on landscape protection.

The strategy has evolved through three phases:
- **2008-2012:** Original "Wakatipu Wilding Conifer Strategy" prepared by Colin Day and Nick Ledgard for QLDC
- **2013-2017:** Second strategic period
- **2023-2033:** Current strategic plan (approved August 2023), emphasising three pillars beyond direct control: community engagement, stakeholder collaboration, and funding security

*Sources: [24], [25], [36]*

### 12.2 Geographic Coverage

The total operational area covers approximately **67,000 hectares** across six management units within the National Wilding Conifer Control Programme:

1. **Whakatipu** — the core Queenstown basin area
2. **Kawarau** — Kawarau Gorge and surrounds
3. **Shotover** — 66,700 ha covering Mt Aurum, Skippers, Macetown
4. **Glenorchy** — head of Lake Wakatipu
5. **Remarkables** — the Remarkables mountain range
6. **Northern Eyres** — northern Eyre Mountains

Key areas include Ben Lomond, One Mile Basin, Queenstown Hill (Te Tapunui), Crown Range, Cecil Peak, Walter Peak, Bayonets, Coronet Peak Station, Skippers, Macetown, Mt Aurum, Wye Creek Catchment, Bobs Peak, and Gibbston Valley.

*Sources: [24], [27], [32]*

### 12.3 Species Targeted in the Wakatipu

Five main conifer species drive the invasion:
- **Douglas fir** (Pseudotsuga menziesii) — the dominant species
- **Contorta pine** (Pinus contorta)
- **Scots pine** (Pinus sylvestris)
- **Larch** (Larix spp.)
- **Corsican pine** (Pinus nigra)

Additional species: Pinus mugo near Gibbston Valley and Pinus radiata on Queenstown Hill.

*Sources: [25]*

### 12.4 Control Techniques

**Ground-based (commercial):**
- Cut stump with herbicide application (accessible trees)
- Drill and fill — holes drilled into trunk, filled with herbicide (trees >10cm diameter on difficult terrain)
- Chainsaw crews and arborist work
- Logging where economically feasible

**Aerial (commercial):**
- **ABBA (Aerial Basal Bark Application / "lancing")** — helicopter-mounted wands chemically ringbark tree trunks; used for scattered trees in inaccessible/steep areas
- **Aerial foliar spray / boom spraying** — used as last resort for dense, inaccessible stands
- Cost comparison: traditional chainsaw removal at **$4,000/ha** over two days vs. aerial spraying at **$650/ha** in nine minutes — an **84% cost reduction**

**Volunteer/hand removal:**
- Hand pulling of seedlings
- Lopping and hand tool clearing (axes, hand saws)
- All trees GPS-marked before removal and added to the national mapping programme

*Sources: [26], [31]*

### 12.5 Funding

**Total spent:** More than **$26 million** since 2009 on wilding control in the Whakatipu area.

**Specific funding allocations:**
- **$5 million over 5 years** — early Wakatipu partnership (DOC committed to conducting all operational work)
- **$100,000/year** — just to hold the line at Cecil Peak alone
- **$2 million** — Soho Property Ltd investment on Mahu Whenua covenants (private landowner contribution)

**National programme funding flowing to the region:**
- 2016-2020: $16 million nationally over 4 years (first phase)
- Budget 2020: $100 million nationally over 4 years
- October 2023: Additional $7 million from DOC budgets
- ORC has urged government to commit $15-25 million annually for the next decade

Each of the six Whakatipu management units submits annual funding bids, with successful funding distributed through the Otago Regional Council.

**Economic case:** The Sapere Research Group cost-benefit analysis found **$1 invested in wilding conifer control today provides an economic benefit worth $96 in 50 years** — a benefit-cost ratio of **20:1 to 34:1**.

*Sources: [24], [31], [33], [37]*

**2025 headline figures:**
- **96,550** pest trees removed
- **605** volunteers contributing **5,000+** hours of ground work

### 12.8 Fire Risk

Wilding pines are a major wildfire hazard in the Wakatipu:

- The **Queenstown Red Zone** (Rat Point to Arthurs Point) was designated after the 2005 Closeburn fire
- Wilding infestation mapping almost exactly matches the most extreme fire risk areas

- The **Lake Ohau wildfire (2020)** burned 5,000+ hectares of largely wilding pine; **$1.2 million** suppression cost; approximately **$35 million** in insurance losses
- QLDC implements reserve closures on Ben Lomond, Queenstown Hill, and Mt Iron based on FENZ fire weather indices
- Removing the Queenstown Hill seed source is a key objective of the Te Tapunui plan partly for wildfire risk reduction

## 13. Future Challenges

1. **Funding gap:** The $100M Budget 2020 allocation expired; current ~$12M/year widely regarded as insufficient. Re-invasion of previously cleared land means past investment at risk without sustained follow-up.

2. **Predator Free 2050 paradox:** Removing exotic mammals (essential for native species) will reduce seed predation on wilding conifers, potentially accelerating spread. Native species provide little seed predation pressure on conifers.

3. **Evolutionary adaptation:** *P. contorta* in NZ produces 3x more filled seeds per cone than in its native range. Hybrid vigour from subspecies mixing may further enhance invasiveness.

4. **Serotiny persistence:** Herbicide-killed trees retain serotinous cone banks with viable seed, enabling reinvasion after treatment.

5. **Ecosystem legacy:** Soil changes from wilding invasion (elevated N and P) promote exotic grass/herb invasion even after pine removal, making tussock restoration unsustainable without ongoing intervention.

6. **Carbon forestry risk:** ETS-driven exotic plantings create new wilding risk if not properly managed under NES-CF.

7. **Scale vs resources:** 7.5 million hectares at risk; only 3 million treated so far (11% of vulnerable land). Control costs increase 30% with each year of delay.

8. **Low public awareness:** Especially among international visitors, reducing social licence for control programmes. Cultural differences in perception (Asian visitors more accepting of conifers).

9. **Douglas fir wave:** Plantings established decades ago have matured and are spreading at alarming rates, creating a new invasion front.

10. **Terrain:** Much affected land is remote, steep, and difficult to access.
